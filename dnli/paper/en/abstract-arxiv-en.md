## Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as judgment assistants and as stand-in participants in psychology experiments, yet little is known about how much their probability estimates move in response to a single new piece of information. We pair 240 premise–hypothesis items from δ-SNLI with one strengthener and one weakener each, elicit the prior and the posteriors in separate calls, and measure the directional movement *s*: the mean of the two posterior changes, each signed by its annotated direction, so that the prior cancels out. We vary whether the update is stated ("Here is something else you learned") or reported ("Someone said that"), and whether the model gives one probability (direct elicitation) or five weighted estimates (multi-estimate elicitation). We test two local models (Qwen3-8B with thinking off and OLMo-2-7B-Instruct) and one commercial model accessed through the codex CLI (120 pairs), with hypotheses and analysis rules fixed before the formal run (not publicly registered). Under stated, direct elicitation all three move in the annotated direction (*s* = 18.6, 20.0 and 25.3 percentage points), although the local models leave 16–29% of updates without any change. Reporting reduces *s* by 2.0, 4.8 and 5.4 points and raises the share of movements in the reverse direction. Multi-estimate elicitation, scored by the weighted mean of the estimates, cuts the weighted endpoint rate (responses ≤5% or ≥95%) from 30%, 42% and 25% to 4%, 5% and 14%, but it also reduces *s* by about 43% and 68% in the local models (2.5 points in the commercial model). An elicitation format that reduces extreme values thus need not preserve sensitivity to evidence; the two should be tested separately. Because δ-NLI annotates only direction, we make no claim about how large an update should be.
