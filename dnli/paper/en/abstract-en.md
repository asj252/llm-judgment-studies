## Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are often used as judgment assistants or as stand-in participants in psychology experiments, but little is known about how they adjust a probability in response to a single new piece of information: existing defeasible-reasoning benchmarks evaluate only direction, and most studies of probability judgment ask only once. We take 240 premise–hypothesis pairs from δ-SNLI, pair each with one strengthener and one weakener, and have models report the prior and the posterior in separate calls. We measure updating by the directional movement *s*: the mean of the posterior change signed by the annotated direction (an increase for a strengthener, a decrease for a weakener, with reverse movement counted as negative), in which the prior cancels out; the unit is percentage points. We manipulate two factors: the update is either stated ("Here is something else you learned") or reported ("Someone said that"); and the model either reports a single probability directly (direct elicitation) or lists five estimates, each with a weight, with the weighted mean as the reported value (multi-estimate elicitation). The model configurations are Qwen3-8B (thinking off) and OLMo-2-7B-Instruct run locally (240 pairs each) and a commercial model called through the codex command-line interface (120 pairs); the hypotheses, the smallest effect sizes of interest (SESOIs), and the analysis rules were frozen before the formal run. Results: (1) under stated presentation and direct elicitation, all three configurations move in the annotated direction, with directional movements of 18.6, 20.0, and 25.3 percentage points, but for the local models 16%–29% of update sentences did not change the reported probability; (2) under reported presentation, directional movement decreases by 2.0, 4.8, and 5.4 points, while the proportion of movements in the reverse direction rises; that is, reporting does not merely make the movement smaller but also makes more items move in the reverse direction; (3) multi-estimate elicitation lowers the endpoint rate, the proportion of reported values at ≤5% or ≥95% (the prior and the two stated posteriors pooled with pre-specified weights), from 30%, 42%, and 25% to 4%, 5%, and 14%, but the directional movement of the two local models also decreases by about 43% and 68%, while that of the commercial model decreases by only 2.5 points. These results indicate that an elicitation format that reduces extreme values does not necessarily preserve sensitivity to evidence, and that the two need to be tested separately. δ-NLI annotates only direction; we do not judge whether the magnitude of the updates is appropriate.
