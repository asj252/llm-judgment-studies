## 5 Discussion

### 5.1 Main findings

Under single-sentence, direction-controlled updates, the probabilities reported by all three model configurations moved in the annotated direction, with a directional movement of roughly 19 to 25 points. When the same update was recast as an anonymous report, the movement became smaller, and at the same time more items moved in the opposite direction. Switching to the elicitation that asks for "5 estimates, each with a weight" sharply reduced endpoint values; but on the two local models it also clearly reduced the response to the update, whereas for codex the reduction was only slight.

### 5.2 Directional sensitivity: the models move, but not every time

H1 shows that these models do not ignore new information: strengtheners raised the probability and weakeners lowered it, and the posterior difference D between strengtheners and weakeners was 37 to 51 points across the three configurations. Item by item, however, 16% to 29% of update sentences left the reported probability unchanged for the local models, and 8% to 9% did so for codex. One possibility is that, under direct elicitation, the models tend to report a few common round numbers; when the prior and the posterior fall on the same round number, no movement can be measured. The between-call variability reported in the Results section (for the local models, two reports for the same prompt differed by 8 to 10 points on average) is also a reminder that a single reported value is coarse, and that conclusions must rest on averages over many pairs.

We do not claim that these movements are "too large" or "too small." δ-NLI annotates only direction and provides no reference for an appropriate magnitude; answering the magnitude question would require people to report probabilities under the same paradigm (see 5.6).

### 5.3 Reporting wording: smaller movement, more reversals

Replacing "Here is something else you learned" with "Someone said that" reduced the directional movement in all three configurations (by 2.0 to 5.4 points). If the report merely led the model to discount the source somewhat, one simple expectation would be a proportional shrinkage of movement in the original direction; but we also observed a rise in the reversal rate (e.g., from 0.16 to 0.22 for olmo2). Following the pre-specified rule, we therefore state this result as "reporting shifts the direction of movement." Discounting as a mechanism was neither tested nor ruled out in this study. For all three configurations the direction is confirmed; only for codex does the point estimate reach the 5-point smallest effect size of interest (SESOI), and for no configuration does the adjusted bound exceed it.

One explanation is pragmatic: in everyday conversation, going out of one's way to say "someone said p" sometimes implies that the speaker does not fully believe p; if the models have learned this pragmatic implication, they may treat the report as expressing some reservation about p, or even as a cue against it. Another explanation is that "Someone said that" turns the update into the event of "someone having said something," so that the model instead reasons about the speech situation rather than about the content of the update. Linguistic work on the extent to which "X said that p" licenses p (Ross & Pavlick, 2019; Jiang & de Marneffe, 2019) is compatible with both explanations, but the dependent variable in that work is an entailment judgment, not the probability movement for the same hypothesis.

Broken down by update type, the three configurations show different patterns: for olmo2 the difference lies almost entirely in strengtheners, whereas for codex it lies mainly in weakeners. This is a post hoc description, and no per-type test was conducted; it suggests that reporting may act in different ways on different models, which merits testing with more reporting wordings (e.g., named sources, sources of differing credibility).

### 5.4 Multi-estimate elicitation: de-extremizing and desensitizing

Multi-estimate elicitation achieved one of its expected effects: endpoint values fell sharply (for the local models, the weighted endpoint rate dropped from about 30%–42% to about 5%). This matters for research on probability updating: if the prior is already reported as 100%, a strengthener has no room to move it upward.

On the local models, however, the same elicitation also clearly reduced the directional movement (by about 43% and 68%). Item by item, the change has two sides: most update sentences that produced "no movement at all" under direct elicitation began to move, but a substantial share of them moved in the reverse direction, so the pooled agreement rate neither declined nor rose by much. The picture for codex is different: the endpoint rate fell from 25% to 14%, the directional movement decreased by only about 2.5 points, and the agreement rate rose slightly.

This raises a question our data cannot yet answer: **does multi-estimate elicitation make the models' judgments more "correct," or does it merely make them respond somewhat less to everything?** In δ-NLI, the update **should** push the estimate in a given direction, but there is no reference for an appropriate magnitude, so a smaller average directional movement cannot yet be judged as an improvement or a decline in judgment quality. In other settings, information **should not** move the estimate (e.g., an irrelevant numerical anchor, or a known outcome when estimating the ex ante probability); if multi-estimate elicitation also reduced responses there, it would look like debiasing. Only by placing the two kinds of settings side by side can one tell whether multi-estimate elicitation leads models to "move when they should and stay put when they should not," or whether it simply dampens responses across the board. We list this test as future work (5.6).

The exploratory analysis in the Results section also shows that the problem does not lie only in "averaging pulls dispersed candidates toward the middle": olmo2's top-weighted candidate also moved by only about 5.5 points on average. But readings of this kind were conceived only post hoc, and the three configurations are mutually confounded in model capability, vendor, training method, and CLI harness, so we cannot use them to say anything about "weaker models." Nor can the present data separate two explanations: that listing multiple candidates itself interferes with the model's use of the evidence, or that the format consumes the model's capacity (e.g., among olmo2's parsable responses, only 22%–29% had weights summing to within [0.95, 1.05]).

The original Verbalized Sampling paper discusses gains in diversity and reports that stronger models benefit more (Zhang et al., 2025); the present study measures directional sensitivity, and the two are different dependent variables. Whether the effect of multi-estimate elicitation on directional sensitivity varies with model capability needs to be tested directly, after controlling for vendor and calling method.

### 5.5 Limitations

- **Materials**: δ-SNLI was released in 2020 and may already be present in training corpora; the effect of contamination on each condition is unknown. The direction of an update reflects the writer's intent; in the original paper, about 8.9% of weakeners were judged by validators to be strengtheners. Our direction spot check involved only one human rater (the author), covered 20 items, and examined only the stated version. The premises come from image captions and tend to be short.
- **Wording**: Reporting used only one wording ("Someone said that"), and the lead-in sentence of the stated version itself signals that the information is true; H2 compares the total effect of these two wordings, and its conclusions are limited to these two wordings.
- **Measurement**: The prior and the posterior come from two independent calls; Δ is "the difference between the probabilities reported under two inputs," not a revision within a single reasoning process. Single reported values from the local models are noisy. Parsing rule v2 was revised after seeing the format failures on the development set (the probability values were not examined during the revision).
- **Models**: There are only three configurations; capability, vendor, training method, and calling method are mutually confounded. codex was called through the codex CLI harness whose system prompt is outside our control, so conclusions apply only to "the model within the harness." The two local models used the same set of sampling parameters, and the effect of settings such as temperature was not tested.
- **Reference**: Without human probabilities under the same paradigm, we cannot judge whether movement is excessive or insufficient.

### 5.6 Future work

1. **Human reference**: Ask people to report priors and posteriors on the same set of pairs (multiple raters per item), to answer the magnitude question and to test whether the reporting wording also "shifts the direction of movement" for humans.
2. **More models**: We plan to extend the same protocol to more models, including different reasoning-effort levels of the same model (e.g., low and high for Gemini 3.8 Flash), so as to compare reasoning effort without changing vendor.
3. **"Should move" versus "should not move"**: Carry multi-estimate elicitation over to classic paradigms in which information should not move the estimate, such as anchoring (an irrelevant number) and hindsight bias (estimating the ex ante probability when the outcome is known), as well as base-rate problems, in which information should be used but is often neglected. Our preliminary literature search did not find studies that apply the "verbalize multiple candidates at once" elicitation to these paradigms.
4. **Variants of reporting**: Named sources, sources of differing credibility, and direct versus indirect quotation, to separate the two explanations of pragmatic implication and source credibility.
