## Tentative title

**Is What You Hear the Same as What You Know? Probability Movement, Reporting Wording, and Multi-Estimate Elicitation of LLMs under Controlled Single-Sentence Updates**

(Tentative English title given in the source: *Hearsay or Fact? Probability Updates of LLMs under Controlled Single-Sentence Evidence*)

---

## 1 Introduction

Everyday reasoning rarely receives all the information at once. When we see that the ground is wet and passers-by are holding umbrellas, we infer that it has rained; if we then hear that a water-splashing festival is being held nearby, this inference should be weakened. A reasonable reasoner judges not only whether new information makes a conclusion "more likely" or "less likely," but also "by how much"; and the same sentence may also carry different weight depending on whether one knows it for certain oneself or it was reported by someone else.

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as judgment assistants, and even as stand-ins for human cognition that simulate participants in psychology experiments [Vasileiou et al. 2026]. Such uses require knowing whether, when a model faces a new piece of information, the probability it reports moves in the right direction, how much it moves, and whether this movement changes with the way the information is presented and the way the question is asked.

Existing research has touched on this problem from several directions, but none has isolated it and measured it.

- **Defeasible-reasoning benchmarks look only at direction.** δ-NLI pairs each premise–hypothesis pair with update sentences that strengthen or weaken the hypothesis [Rudinger et al. 2020]; subsequent work treats it as a classification or ranking task, for example requiring only that the updated probability estimate agree with the annotated direction [Wang et al. 2025, Always Tell Me The Odds]. Whether the direction is correct can be evaluated, but the magnitude of the movement has not been reported.
- **Probabilistic NLI and probabilistic inference ask only once.** UNLI collected human subjective probabilities on a 0–1 scale for SNLI items [Chen et al. 2020]; ProbCOPA compares the distributions of humans and reasoning models on a 0–100 scale and finds that the models rarely give mid-range values and lean toward the two ends [Kamath et al. 2026]. These works all involve a single judgment, with no comparison of "before and after new information appears."
- **Research on forecast updating does not control direction.** EvolveCast has models report a forecast probability once before and once after seeing a news item, with Metaculus community forecasts as the reference [Yuan et al. 2025]. The news is obtained by retrieval, its direction is not controlled, and a single news item may also mix several events.

In this paper, we measure the probability movement of LLMs under **single-sentence updates whose direction is fixed in advance**, and test the effects of two manipulations.

**First, reported wording.** People adjust how much they accept information according to the credibility of its source [Hovland & Weiss 1951; Pornpitakpan 2004]. For LLMs, research on sources has mostly concerned knowledge conflicts and answer selection: models are given two mutually conflicting documents labeled with different sources to see which one they choose [Schuster et al. 2026], or are tested on whether they defer more to an answer labeled as coming from an "expert" [Bajaj & Tiganj 2026]. In linguistics, the extent to which "X said that p" licenses the inference of p is a classic question in research on verb veridicality [Ross & Pavlick 2019; Jiang & de Marneffe 2019], but there the dependent variable is an entailment label or speaker commitment, not the probability movement of another hypothesis. As far as our search found, no study has presented **the same new piece of information** in two wordings, "Here is something else you learned: U" and "Someone said that U", and compared the models' probability movement for the same hypothesis.

**Second, extreme values and multi-estimate elicitation.** When a model is asked directly for a single probability, its answers tend to crowd at the two ends: verbalized confidence mostly falls in 80–100% [Xiong et al. 2024], and reasoning models rarely give mid-range values [Kamath et al. 2026]. For research on updating, this is not merely a calibration problem: if the prior is already reported as 100%, strengthening information has no room to move it upward, and whether the model was influenced by the sentence cannot be measured. Verbalized Sampling (VS) proposes that, instead of having the model give only one answer, the model list several candidates at once, each labeled with a probability, to alleviate the problem of models always giving the most typical answer [Zhang et al. 2025]; research on question-answering calibration has also found that giving several candidates at once, or a verbalized probability distribution, can reduce calibration error for some models and datasets [Tian et al. 2023; Xiong et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2025b]. As far as our search found, we have not yet found a study that, in single-sentence, direction-controlled probability updating, tests the effects of such elicitation formats on both endpoint values and directional sensitivity.

We sample 240 premise–hypothesis pairs from δ-SNLI (240 distinct images), take one strengthener and one weakener for each pair, and collect probabilities from seven model configurations: Qwen3-8B (thinking off) and OLMo-2-7B-Instruct run locally (240 pairs each); the commercial model gpt-6.1-sol called through the codex command-line interface (120 pairs); and, in an extension run with the same protocol, gpt-oss-120b, claude-sonnet-5.5 and gemini-3.8-flash at two reasoning efforts, called through Google's agy command-line interface (the same 120 pairs). For each item, the model reports, in separate calls, the prior and the posteriors under two presentations: stated ("Here is something else you learned") and reported ("Someone said that"). There are two elicitation formats: reporting a single probability directly (direct elicitation), or listing five estimates, each labeled with a weight (multi-estimate elicitation). The main measure is the **directional movement** *s*: the average of the increase under strengtheners and the decrease under weakeners, in which the prior cancels out. The hypotheses, the smallest effect sizes of interest (SESOIs; 5 points for probability movement and 10 percentage points for differences in endpoint rate), the handling of missing data, and the statistical methods were frozen before the formal set was run and kept unchanged for the extension; the statistics use a cluster bootstrap (resampling images) and Holm correction within each configuration.

Our main results are:

1. **All seven configurations move, but not every time.** Under stated presentation and direct elicitation, the directional movement is between 17.6 and 28.3 points; but counted per update sentence, 16%–29% of the update sentences did not change the reported probability for the local models, and 7%–15% for the configurations accessed through agent harnesses.
2. **Reporting shifts the direction of movement, a little.** When the update is changed to "Someone said," the point estimate of the directional movement becomes smaller in all seven configurations (by 0.9 to 5.4 points; the direction is confirmed in six), while the proportion of movements in the reverse direction rises in all seven (for example, the reversal rate of OLMo-2 goes from 0.16 to 0.22). This cannot simply be read as "discounting" in the same direction. Only the point estimate of codex reaches 5 points.
3. **Multi-estimate elicitation de-extremizes; in the local models it also desensitizes.** The proportion of reported values at the endpoints (≤5% or ≥95%), computed with the pre-specified condition weights and with the weighted mean as the reported value under multi-estimate elicitation, drops in all seven configurations (e.g., from 30%, 42%, and 25% to 4%, 5%, and 14% in the original three); the directional movement of the two local models also decreases by about 43% and 68%, while in the five harnessed configurations the point estimates lie between −3.7 and +0.5 points.

Our contributions are:

1. **A controlled update paradigm and materials**: single-sentence updates with annotated direction, with strengtheners and weakeners in pairs; two numerical probabilities for the same hypothesis, before and after; a directional movement measure in which the prior cancels out; and worst-case imputation bounds for missing units. The main design and analysis rules were frozen before the formal run; the only deviation after the formal run of the original three configurations (Addendum A1), and the events during the extension, are described in the Methods section.
2. **A test of reporting wording**: when the same information is changed to an anonymous report, the models' movement becomes smaller and reverse movement increases; this pattern is consistent in direction across seven configurations, small in size, and differs across configurations when broken down by update type.
3. **Multi-estimate elicitation applied to updating**: we test the format "list five estimates and weight them" in single-sentence, direction-controlled probability updating, find that while reducing endpoint values it can substantially reduce directional sensitivity to evidence in some models but not in others, and raise the issue that "de-extremizing" and "desensitizing" need to be tested separately.
4. **A behavioral diagnostic for elicitation formats**: because the protocol fixes the direction of the evidence and measures prior and posterior separately, it yields a per-model response profile to an elicitation format: how much the format reduces extreme values, what it costs in sensitivity to evidence, and whether the spread of the elicited candidates behaves like an expression of uncertainty. Across seven configurations these profiles differ markedly, which suggests that formats such as Verbalized Sampling should be checked model by model before their outputs are read as calibrated judgments.

We do **not** claim that the models "under-update" or "over-update": δ-NLI annotates only direction and provides no human reference for the appropriate magnitude. A human probability reference under the same paradigm is left for future work.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Defeasible reasoning and probabilistic natural language inference

δ-NLI casts defeasible reasoning as a task over (P, H, U) triples: given a premise P and a hypothesis H, judge whether an update U makes H more likely (strengthening) or less likely (weakening). The data comprise three subsets, SNLI, ATOMIC, and Social Chemistry, validated by human annotators on a 5-point Likert scale; no numerical probabilities were collected [Rudinger et al. 2020]. On δ-SNLI, GPT-4o already reaches a direction-classification accuracy of 92.6%, higher than the human accuracy reported in the original paper [2502.08080].

UNLI relabels a portion of SNLI with 0–1 subjective probabilities: each pair is rated by 2–3 people with a slider and the ratings are averaged, with an inter-rater correlation of about 0.70 [Chen et al. 2020]. Always Tell Me The Odds trains probability estimation on UNLI and then uses δ-NLI to constrain the direction of the estimates, evaluating with ranking accuracy on δ-SNLI; it does not report the probability difference before and after the update for the same item [Wang et al. 2025]. In a pilot study, BIRD found that it was difficult to reach consensus when people were asked to report probabilities directly, and therefore switched to pairwise preference judgments [Feng et al. 2025]. ProbCOPA collects 0–100 ratings from 25–30 people for each of 210 COPA items and finds that human distributions are graded, whereas 8 reasoning models lean toward the two ends; there is no significant difference between high and low thinking budgets; and adjusting temperature or persona prompts has limited effect [Kamath et al. 2026].

Another line of work studies belief revision when a rule is overturned by a counterexample. Belief-R uses three-option multiple-choice items to examine whether models can retract a conclusion after a new premise appears [Wilie et al. 2024]. Vasileiou et al. compare humans and three frontier models on everyday scenarios of the form "rule + instance + counterexample"; the dependent variables are the proportion of revising the rule versus doubting the instance, and whether the revision generalizes to related beliefs; the proportion with which the models target the refuted rule is similar to that of humans, but they almost never generalize [Vasileiou et al. 2026]. These works are concerned with "which one to revise," not with the magnitude of probability movement.

**Difference from this paper**: we adopt the direction annotations of δ-NLI, but we measure the numerical probability of the same hypothesis before and after the update and its movement, rather than direction classification or a single probability.

### 2.2 Probability updating in LLMs

EvolveCast is closest to our work: on Metaculus questions, it has models report a probability once before and once after a news item, obtaining 1,613 question–news pairs, and uses the community forecast's values at the two time points as the reference [Yuan et al. 2025]. The differences are that the news is obtained by retrieval and its direction is not controlled; there is no manipulation of source or wording; and elicitation formats are not compared. The authors state that the models' update magnitudes tend to be too small, but the main text reports metrics such as MSE and direction agreement rate and does not directly compare the magnitude of movement between models and humans.

Other work examines updating from the perspective of Bayesian coherence. Imran et al. read priors, likelihoods, and posteriors from token log-probabilities (rather than verbalized probabilities), use a Bayesian Coherence Coefficient (BCC) to measure pretrained models from five families without instruction tuning, and find that the coefficient is highly correlated with the logarithm of the parameter count (r = 0.906) [2507.17951]. Kim et al. compare models' verbalized confidence on SciQ under no-evidence and with-evidence conditions; one of their manipulations of evidence strength is source credibility (a peer-reviewed paper published by a university professor vs. an anonymous blog post); all of their evidence supports the correct answer, and there is no weakening direction [Kim et al. 2025]. LEAP has models give a likelihood for each piece of evidence separately, and then combines them into a posterior using an explicit prior and a deterministic model; the posterior is not self-reported by the model [Chen et al. 2026].

**Difference from this paper**: in this paper, the new information is a single sentence with annotated direction, strengtheners and weakeners come in pairs, and the same model self-reports two probabilities, before and after, for the same hypothesis.

### 2.3 Sources, reporting, and evidential markers

**Linguistics: verb veridicality and speaker commitment.** "X said that p" and "X knows that p" commit to p to different degrees, a classic difficulty for NLI. Ross & Pavlick test NLI models with 1,500 sentence pairs and 137 verbs and find that both humans and BERT tend to treat verbs as veridical [Ross & Pavlick 2019]; CommitmentBank annotates how speaker commitment varies in negation, modal, question, and conditional environments [de Marneffe et al. 2019], and Jiang & de Marneffe evaluate NLI models on it [Jiang & de Marneffe 2019]; embedding non-factive verbs or evidential adverbs also disrupts the entailment judgments of LLMs [Basmov et al. 2024]. The dependent variable in these works is an entailment label or a commitment rating, and they ask "whether p can be inferred from the report"; we ask "how a reported U moves the probability of another hypothesis H."

**Epistemic and evidential markers in prompts.** Adding markers such as "I've heard it's" or "I'm sure" to a question changes the answer accuracy of models [Zhou et al. 2023]. Belém et al. use the template "Speaker believes it is [uncertainty expression] that S" to have models report a 0–100 probability from the speaker's perspective, comparing 14 uncertainty expressions; the study uses only wordings attributed to the speaker and has no non-attributed control [Belém et al. 2024]. Du et al. classify belief expressions into 17 types (including evidentiality) and examine whether models follow the context or their own priors [Du et al. 2026].

**Source credibility and LLMs.** In knowledge conflicts, models prefer institutional sources over individuals and social media, but this preference reverses once a claim from a low-credibility source is repeated multiple times [Schuster et al. 2026]. That study presents two conflicting documents labeled with sources at the same time and measures preference by option-token probabilities, without having the model self-report probabilities. Models defer more readily to a previous answer labeled as coming from a "human expert" [Bajaj & Tiganj 2026]; in a large-scale source-discernment test, the models' ability to discern source reliability is close to chance, and their reliance on source popularity is about twice their reliance on reliability [Ashkinaze et al. 2026]; in a Turkish evidential-suffix task, humans show a stable source-credibility effect, whereas the effect in LLMs is unstable and often reversed [Karakaş & Şimşek 2026].

**Human research.** People discount untrustworthy sources at the time they receive the information, and this discount fades over time [Hovland & Weiss 1951]; for a review of fifty years of source-credibility research, see Pornpitakpan [2004]. Harris et al. model the expertise and trustworthiness of experts with Bayesian networks and test the model with numerical ratings of proposition credibility [Harris et al. 2016].

**Difference from this paper**: existing LLM studies manipulate source identity, source preference, or uncertainty expressions, and their dependent variables are mostly answer selection, accuracy, or a single probability. We hold the content of the new information fixed and change only "learned" to "someone said," comparing the probability movement of the same hypothesis in both the strengthening and the weakening directions; our results show that this change not only makes the movement smaller but also increases reverse movement (Section 4.3).

### 2.4 Verbalized probability, extreme responses, and multi-estimate elicitation

The verbalized confidence of LLMs is often too high: Xiong et al. find that confidence mostly falls in 80–100% and is mostly a multiple of 5; having the model give Top-K guesses with their probabilities reduces calibration error [Xiong et al. 2024]. Tian et al. find on GPT-4 that giving 4 candidates with probabilities at once yields a lower ECE than giving only 1 on SciQ and TruthfulQA, but a higher one on TriviaQA [Tian et al. 2023]. Having the model verbalize a probability distribution over candidate answers that sums to 1 also lowered the Brier score and ECE on several closed-ended datasets, with smaller models benefiting more, but there are also tasks where the effect was worse (for example, on the mathematical reasoning dataset OlympiadBench, both the Brier score and the ECE of Qwen3-4B with the distribution method are worse than with verbalized confidence) [Wang et al. 2025b]. Other research treats listing multiple candidates at once, each with a confidence, as a baseline and finds that the candidates' confidences often sum to more than 1 and that overconfidence remains [Wang & Stengel-Eskin 2026]. In opinion-distribution tasks, distributions reported directly in words are closer to population distributions than distributions read from log-probs [Meister et al. 2024].

Verbalized Sampling has a model generate k responses at once, each labeled with a probability, to alleviate the "mode collapse" in which aligned models always give the most typical answer, and it mainly evaluates generation diversity [Zhang et al. 2025]. Its appendix examines the calibration of verbalized probabilities: on closed answer spaces such as state names, countries, and programming languages, it compares the correlation between each answer's verbalized probability and its corpus frequency. The calibration target there is "the relative frequency with which each answer occurs," not "the probability that an event holds"; endpoint values are not counted, and there are no two judgments, before and after.

Psychology has related approaches: having people assign probabilities to every interval across the whole range of values reduces overprecision [Haran et al. 2010]; averaging two estimates from the same person is more accurate than a single estimate [Vul & Pashler 2008]; assuming that the first estimate was wrong before giving a second estimate yields lower error after averaging [Herzog & Hertwig 2009]; in two-alternative judgments, averaging the same person's two confidence ratings improves the accuracy of confidence judgments more than taking the higher of the two [Litvinova et al. 2020]. However, the two estimates in these "inner crowd" studies are separated in time and relatively independent, whereas multi-estimate elicitation produces them in a single generation with candidates that depend on one another, so the two should not be directly equated.

**Difference from this paper**: existing work applies multi-candidate or verbalized-distribution elicitation to the calibration of one-shot question answering or to generation diversity; we apply it to before-and-after judgments of "the probability that something holds in a scenario", and test its effects on both endpoint values and directional sensitivity. We do not require the five estimates to differ from one another, so as not to create dispersion artificially; if a model gives five nearly identical estimates, that is itself a result.
